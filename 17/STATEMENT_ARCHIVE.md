## aBaKada

Source: `abakada/index.html`

> The Filipino alphabet has 28 letters. At its prime positions — 2, 3,
> 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 — sit B, C, E, G, K, M, O, Q and U: enough
> to speak with. buko, kubo, kuko, ube, buo, oo, mo, ko. Even grammar
> survives the sieve: the infix -um- is built entirely of prime
> letters, so the poem can conjugate — buko, bumuko, bumubuko. Two of
> the nine, C and Q, arrived only in 1987, added for borrowed words,
> and they flash only when English drifts in: queue, become, cue.
> The poem absorbs the way the language always has, conjugating the
> arrivals — qumuqueue, bumecome, umemu — until borrowed and born are
> one tongue, and only the strip remembers where a letter is from. NG,
> the most Filipino letter of all, sits at position 16 = 2 to the 4th
> and stays dark. The title is the alphabet’s own name run through the
> sieve, keeping what is prime.

## Prime Song

Source: `prime-song/index.html`

> A glossolalic song in a scale no one has heard before: seventeen
> pitches drawn at random from the human vocal range. The voice is
> additive, built only from prime partials (1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 of the
> fundamental); its mouth is two formants sitting on prime multiples of
> 55 Hz, and the glyph you read is derived from that prime pair — height
> from the first formant, backness from the second. Consonants are noise
> at prime multiples of 470 Hz. Verses run 3, 5, or 7 lines of 3, 5, or
> 7 syllables. Nothing here is divisible; everything is sung.

## Same Time Tomorrow

Source: `same-time-tomorrow/index.html`

> The iroha contains each of forty-seven historical kana exactly once.
> This clock begins with the old poem, then reads it by an offset and a
> stride. An English translation fades during the first revolution.
> Every forty-seven ticks the poem reaches the same first character with
> a new stride. Its first returns replace ゐ and ゑ with い and え. On
> each return thereafter, the next prime selects a kana by its residue
> modulo 47, and a random kana is grafted over it. Each sign keeps two
> grafts; the oldest is shed when another arrives. After 47 × 46 = 2,162
> ticks, the order returns. The alphabet does not.
