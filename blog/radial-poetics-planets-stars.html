The Radial Poetics of Planets and Stars

By Xyh Tamura · September 03, 2026 · xyhtamura.github.io

The mathematical vision of the planets and stars as layered spheres has not only shaped scientific understanding but also transformed our relationship to scale.

All meaningful human life occurs in Earth's negligibly thin crust, where spherical curvature is practically irrelevant and space is locally flat. Beneath and beyond it, however, lie vast and invisible strata that science renders through cross-sections and simulations: abstract images that extend perception far below and above what is sensorially available. The canonical version of that rendering is the Preliminary Reference Earth Model, which gives density, pressure and seismic velocity as functions of radius alone.1 A world becomes a stack of shells, and everything that is not depth is averaged away.

This cross-sectioning and radial imagination of the earth, and by extension, other planets and stars, has become a cultural technology for imagining their interiors. Popular media creates experiential scaffolding for these scales, from Jules Verne's Journey to the Center of the Earth to The Core, contemporary digital visualizations of planetary descents, games like Minecraft or Starbound, as well as ascensions like space travel and high-altitude photography. This shows how scientific models don't merely describe reality but actively produce epistemic frameworks that mediate our relationships to spaces.

Drawing on post-phenomenology,2 Cold War science studies, and my own experience as a Filipino physicist who modeled spherical waveguides whose structure emerged from naval acoustics, I trace how this radial imagination emerged from specific historical and geopolitical contexts. This radial vision of worlds was built on and furthered through Cold War militarism. It made planets legible with models from submarine sonar detection, seismic monitoring of nuclear tests, and resource extraction.

The dates are close together and the funding is traceable. The deep sound channel was established by wartime work on long-range signalling, where the ocean's own stratification was found to carry an explosion for hundreds of miles.3 Project Vela Uniform, begun to detect and identify Soviet underground tests, raised annual federal support for US seismology by more than a factor of thirty and turned a small academic discipline into an academic-military-industrial one.4 What the Navy paid for is legible in what oceanography went on to know and not know.5 The layered interior is not a neutral inheritance from these programmes. It is the shape a world takes when the question being asked of it is whether something is hiding inside.

These models and depictions are crafted poetic technologies and they function as cognitive prostheses that extend human perception into otherwise inaccessible scales. We can now "know" the Earth's core or stellar fusion layers. But it is one world-making apparatus among many, and like all tools, can be and has been repurposed beyond the extractive and martial contexts it has been used in.
